**Abstract:** The pursuit of general-purpose robotic manipulation is hindered by the scarcity of diverse, real-world interaction data. Unlike the large-scale web vision-language data, robotic data collection is an active process incurring prohibitive physical costs. As a result, the key challenge is not merely to collect more robot data, but to obtain training data that provides informative learning knowledge by broadening behavioral coverage and capturing meaningful variations in objects, scenes, and skills. However, **how to automatically curate task proposals that maximize the utility of collected robot data remains a critical yet under-explored challenge.** To address this, we introduce **RoboGene** , an agentic framework designed to automate the generation of diverse and informative manipulation tasks across heterogeneous robotic embodiments, spanning single-arm, dual-arm, and mobile robots. RoboGene integrates three core components: (1) diversity-driven sampling for broad task coverage, (2) agentic self-reflection for task-quality assessment, and (3) human-verified feedback memory for executiongrounded validation and continual refinement. We conduct extensive quantitative analysis and large-scale real-world experiments, collecting datasets of 18k trajectories and introducing novel metrics to assess task quality in terms of feasibility and diversity. Results demonstrate that RoboGene significantly outperforms frontier foundation models (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro). Furthermore, realworld experiments show that VLA models trained on data generated by RoboGene achieve higher success rates and stronger generalization, highlighting the importance of high-quality task generation. Our project page and dataset are publicly available at robogene-boost-vla.github.io, with dataset repositories hosted on Hugging Face and ModelScope. 

**Keywords:** Robot Learning, Vision-Language-Action Models, Task Generation

Foundation models trained on web-scale data have shown strong generalization across diverse tasks [1, 2], motivating the community to build general-purpose embodied agents for manipulation in unstructured environments [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. The prevalent approach [10, 13, 14] relies on human operators to specify tasks by hand, which is hard to scale and prone to cognitive bias. Designers favor simple primitives such as “pick the apple” over complex, multi-stage interactions, leaving the data severely long-tailed and limiting generalization to novel instructions and scenes. This raises a central question. _How can we automate the design of high-quality and diverse collection tasks to improve pre-training and generalist capability?_ 

A natural remedy is to use Large Language Models (LLMs) for automated task generation [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20], which improves scalability but raises new obstacles. First, without access to global dataset statistics, an LLM cannot reshape the skewed distribution toward a more balanced one, and therefore fails to correct the long-tail effect. Second, LLMs lack physical grounding and are prone to hallucination, often producing tasks that violate user constraints or reference non-existent objects. They also cannot judge whether a given robot embodiment is able to execute a generated instruction, so guaranteeing consistency and feasibility still demands heavy expert effort. Finally, the generation is typically open-loop and ignores real-world execution feedback, such as object substitution or motion failure, that could refine later generations. 

In this paper, we present **RoboGene** , an agentic framework for the automated, large-scale generation of diverse, physically grounded, and high-quality manipulation tasks. To counter the long-tail problem, we adopt a diversity-driven sampling scheme based on a Least Frequently Used (LFU) strategy that steers the agent toward under-explored parts of the task space and prioritizes rare objects and skills. To suppress hallucination, a self-reflection module routes each task proposal through three specialized evaluators that independently check constraint adherence, novelty, and embodied plausibility before human verification. A long-term memory module further consolidates Humanin-the-Loop (HITL) feedback, assimilating corrections from task edits and execution failures during real-world collection, thereby enabling subsequent task generation to produce more robot-executable tasks. Together, these components substantially reduce expert labor while keeping the resulting task distribution both executable and statistically balanced. 

We validate RoboGene through quantitative analysis and large-scale real-world experiments. We design a suite of metrics that capture both per-task quality, such as physical feasibility, and global dataset properties, such as object and skill diversity. RoboGene clearly outperforms strong foundation models such as GPT-4o [21] and Gemini 2.5 Pro [22], as well as manual design. For real-world validation, we collect over 18k trajectories spanning 1200 distinct tasks with 15 demonstrations each to benchmark VLA pre-training. A _π_ 0 [23] policy pre-trained on RoboGene-generated tasks generalizes better to unseen scenarios, including novel objects, background changes, static distractors,

### **2 Related Work** 

#### **2.1 Generalist Vision-Language-Action Models** 

Large-scale robotic datasets [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 24, 25, 26] have made it possible to move beyond specialized manipulation policies [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34] toward generalist Vision-LanguageAction (VLA) models [3, 5, 23, 35]. Recent work further improves VLA architectures with stronger open-world generalization, diffusion or autoregressive action modeling, multisensory grounding, spatial reasoning, and cross-embodiment representations [8, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Complementary to model-side progress, data-centric studies show that generalization depends not only on the amount of robot data, but also on its coverage across environments, objects, and tasks [41, 42]. In particular, increasing demonstrations for the same task can yield diminishing returns, whereas broader task and environment diversity can improve downstream adaptation. These findings shift the key bottleneck from simply collecting more trajectories to deciding which tasks should be collected under limited and expensive robot time. However, existing large-scale datasets are still largely shaped by humanspecified tasks and therefore inherit long-tail biases toward common objects, scenes, and primitive skills. RoboGene addresses this data-utility problem by automatically generating diverse, physically grounded task specifications for VLA pre-training. 

#### **2.2 Automated Task Generation** 

Automated task generation has evolved from procedural task templates [43] to LLM-driven systems that generate task descriptions, simulation environments, and reward functions [15, 16, 17, 18, 44]. This direction has been extended to curriculum design [45, 46], realistic task and scene synthesis [47, 48, 49, 50], and sim-to-real data generation [19, 20, 51]. Despite these advances, most existing methods treat task generation as a component of simulation or benchmark construction, rather than as a closed-loop data curation problem for real-world VLA pre-training. They usually do not explicitly balance the global distribution of generated tasks, and open-loop generation can still produce hallucinated assets, incompatible object-skill pairs, or instructions that exceed the capability of a given robot embodiment. In contrast, RoboGene combines diversity-driven sampling, self-reflective feasibility checking, and human-verified execution feedback through long-term memory. This enables generated tasks to be not only semantically varied, but also executable and useful as real-world data-collection targets for pre-training generalist policies.

#### **3.2 Diversity-Driven Task Space Sampling** 

To counter the long-tail task distribution and ensure balanced coverage, we adopt a Least Frequently Used (LFU) sampling strategy guided by historical statistics _H_ . We maintain a usage counter _u_ ( _·_ ) for every scenario, object, and skill, incrementing it whenever a valid task is generated, so that sampling progressively favors under-explored elements. Each generation cycle _t_ proceeds hierarchically: we first select the least-used scenario _et_ = arg min _e∈E u_ ( _e_ ), and then sample objects and skills conditioned on _et_ . To avoid incoherent pairings such as folding a microwave in a factory, we retain only

a relevant subset _Oet ⊂O_ whose cosine similarity to the scenario description, measured by a text encoder, exceeds a threshold _θ_ = 0 _._ 7 calibrated on manually labeled scenario–object pairs. From this subset we draw a fixed-size candidate set _Ot_ via LFU, and obtain the skill set _St_ in the same manner. The resulting tuple ( _et, Ot, St_ ) is then inserted into the prompt as context, since providing a candidate set rather than a single forced choice allows the agent to preserve diversity. 

#### **3.3 Self-Reflection and Task Generation** 

Upon determining the sampling constraints ( _et, Ot, St_ ), the framework enters a generator-evaluatorimprovement loop to produce a high-quality task specification _T_ . We present prompt templates for each node in Appendix I. 

**Task Proposal Generation.** The initial task _T_ raw is synthesized by a Proposal Generator _G_ ( _·_ ), implemented via a Large Language Model (LLM). We construct a structured prompt injecting the sampled constraints, robot type _r_ , and agent role definitions. For embodiments requiring spatial awareness, such as mobile manipulators, _G_ ( _·_ ) is instantiated as a Vision-Language Model (VLM). This allows the system to process scene images _{I_ 1 _, . . . , Ik}_ as supplementary input, grounding the generated task instructions in the physical layout to ensure spatial plausibility. The output _T_ raw is formatted as a standardized JSON containing the robot type, scenario category, task description, object, and skill lists. 

**Multi-Faceted Self-Reflection.** Because the raw proposal _T_ raw may contain hallucinations or infeasible steps, we route it through a set of specialized LLM-based evaluators. Each returns a naturallanguage critique, and a refiner then merges these critiques into a revised task. 

The Embodied Plausibility Evaluator _E_ emb( _·_ ) judges whether the task can be carried out given the robot embodiment _r_ , checking the consistency among robot capabilities, object affordances, and action order. It flags embodiment-specific issues, such as a single arm attempting a task that needs a stabilizing hand, two arms taking incoherent roles, or a mobile robot acting before reaching the target. When a step is infeasible or ambiguous, it suggests a fix, for example clarifying arm roles, adding stabilization, or splitting a coupled action. We denote its critique by _f_ emb = _E_ emb( _T_ raw _, r_ ). 

The Novelty Evaluator _E_ nov( _·_ ) rates difficulty and novelty along four dimensions, namely object complexity (e.g., deformable objects or fluids), contact precision (e.g., in-hand manipulation or tool use), temporal and logical depth (e.g., long-horizon tasks with partial observability), and environment structure (e.g., dynamic targets or disturbances). Its feedback _f_ nov = _E_ nov( _T_ raw) ensures that the task is sufficiently novel. 

The Constraint Adherence Evaluator _E_ con( _·_ ) checks that the task matches the assigned scene and uses only objects and skills from the sampled sets _Ot_ and _St_ , without introducing hallucinated items. Its feedback _f_ con = _E_ con( _T_ raw _, Ot, St_ ) suggests edits to the task context. Finally, the Self-Reflection Refiner _E_ ref( _·_ ) merges the three critiques with the raw proposal to refine. 


#### **3.4 Integrating Human Feedback via Memory** 

To enable continuous system evolution and prevent the recurrence of errors, we integrate a Humanin-the-Loop (HITL) mechanism coupled with a semantic Long-Term Memory module _M_ . 

**Human-in-the-Loop Feedback Consolidation.** When tasks are dispatched for real-world execution, human operators provide feedback not just as binary labels, but as rich natural language explanations detailing infeasibility reasons (e.g., "writing number ’2’ fails on opposite-facing arms due to spatial mapping inversion"). We employ an LLM-based summarizer to periodically distill these specific feedback instances into generalized heuristic knowledge which is stored in _M_ as key-value pairs ( _K, V_ ), where the key is the semantic embedding of the task context and the value is the actionable guideline.

### **4 Experiments** 

We evaluate RoboGene by addressing three pivotal research questions regarding the validity, diversity, and utility of the generated tasks. Specifically, we investigate: (1) whether individual tasks are linguistically comprehensible and physically executable; (2) if the collective dataset exhibits sufficient coverage across scenarios, objects, and skills; and (3) the effectiveness of the collected data for downstream policy pre-training. To this end, we benchmark RoboGene against human experts, the rule-based method, and state-of-the-art Large Foundation Models (LFMs), specifically GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Pro. For each method, we generated a suite of 900 tasks spanning single-arm, dual-arm, and mobile manipulation domains, comprising 300 tasks for each robot category. We provide the implementation details for the baseline methods in Appendix E. 

#### **4.1 Individual Task Evaluation** 

**Evaluation Metrics.** Quantifying the quality of open-ended robotic tasks remains an open challenge. We introduce six metrics to assess semantic and physical validity. _Task Clarity_ and _Type Consistency_ check for linguistic clarity and robot type alignment (e.g., dual-arm constraints). _Logical Validity_ ensures semantic plausibility, penalizing contextually inappropriate actions (e.g., industrial assembly in a kitchen). To measure grounding accuracy, we define _Object Coverage_ and _Skill Coverage_ as the ratio of generated entities present in the valid configuration sets _O_ and _S_ , respectively. Finally, _Physical Feasibility_ reports the average success rate of tasks executed via human teleoperation (5 trials per task). To reduce evaluator-specific bias, Task Clarity, Type Consistency,

and Logical Validity are independently assessed by human evaluators and Claude Opus 5. We report the average in the main text and individual results in Appendix D. 

**Evaluation Results.** Table 1 demonstrates that RoboGene establishes a new state-of-the-art across all dimensions. While standard LFMs like GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Pro exhibit high task clarity, their coverage is fundamentally bottlenecked by severe hallucinations and limited long-tail diversity. Specifically, over 14.9% of GPT-4o’s and 52.0% of Gemini’s generated objects are physically absent from the valid library (see Appendix D for exact fraction details). In contrast, RoboGene effectively grounds all the generation within the available assets, achieving an object coverage of 0.6323 and a skill coverage of 0.9153. Furthermore, our self-reflection mechanism ensures physical grounding, outperforming the logical validity and physical feasibility of human-designed tasks, whereas rulebased baselines fail to produce coherent, physically executable behaviors.

#### **4.3 Real-World Manipulation Task Evaluation** 

In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness of our pipeline through extensive real-world experiments. We first validate the feasibility of the generated tasks on physical robots. Subsequently, we assess the contribution of RoboGene to large-scale pre-training and its generalization capabilities. 

**Validation of Generated Tasks.** To verify the physical feasibility of our task generation pipeline, we conducted single-task evaluations on three distinct robots: a single-arm UR-5e, a dual-arm Franka, and an AgileX mobile robot. For each robot type, we generated three representative tasks using RoboGene and collected 250 trajectories per task. We train ACT [53] for the stationary robots and _π_ 0 _._ 5 [8] for the mobile robot and report the average success rates over 20 rollouts for each task. The system achieved robust performance, including a 90% success rate on the SUR-SortButton task. The detailed experimental setups and quantitative success rates are deferred to Appendix F. 

**Effectiveness of Pre-training Datasets** . We constructed a dataset of 150 single-arm and 150 dualarm tasks generated from each method to investigate the impact of large-scale pre-training on downstream performance. As shown in Figure 6, we pre-trained the _π_ 0 [23] on these datasets and subsequently fine-tuned them on five unseen dual-arm tasks with 15 demonstrations per task, reporting the average success rate over 20 rollouts. Our results indicate a significant performance disparity between RoboGene and LLM-based baselines. Policies pre-trained on data designed by LFMs (GPT-4o, Gemini) struggled to adapt to new tasks, often yielding success rates below 20% or failing completely. In contrast, the comparison between RoboGene-15 and 30-epoch baselines suggests that RoboGene pre-training enables faster downstream adaptation, reaching comparable or better performance with fewer fine-tuning epochs.

### **5 Conclusion and Limitations** 

We introduce RoboGene, an agentic framework that automates the generation of diverse and physically grounded manipulation tasks, addressing the data diversity bottleneck in robotic learning. By pairing a diversity-driven LFU sampling strategy with a multi-faceted self-reflection mechanism, RoboGene mitigates the hallucinations of large foundation models. Experiments show that it yields substantially higher object and skill coverage than state-of-the-art baselines, and trains VLA policies with stronger robustness to unseen objects, backgrounds, lighting, and instruction shifts. We open-source our resources to support future research. 

**Limitations.** RoboGene relies on predefined scenario, object, and skill libraries, which enable controllable generation and systematic diversity sampling but also bound the task space to their coverage. Tasks involving unseen objects or skills thus require manual library expansion. Future work will explore automatic expansion from robot logs, scene observations, and web-scale knowledge, enlarging RoboGene’s task space while preserving physical grounding and controllable diversity.

### **A Implementation Details of RoboGene** 

**Framework Overview.** RoboGene operates as a closed-loop agentic framework designed to autonomously generate high-quality, physically grounded, and diverse robotic manipulation tasks within real-world environments. The framework is founded upon three synergistic components devised to address the inherent challenges of automated data generation. First, we employ a diversitydriven sampling mechanism based on a Least Frequently Used (LFU) strategy to mitigate the longtail distribution problem often observed in robotic datasets. This mechanism actively steers the generative agent toward under-explored regions of the task space, prioritizing interactions with rarely utilized objects and skills. Second, to improve the quality of the tasks, we incorporate a selfreflection mechanism. This module utilizes three specialized evaluators built on foundation models to independently assess constraint adherence, novelty, and Embodied Plausibility. These evaluators rigorously scrutinize generated task proposals to provide actionable feedback. Third, the framework features a long-term memory module that consolidates Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) feedback. By assimilating corrections derived from task modifications and execution failures encountered during real-world collection, the system progressively refines its understanding of physical constraints and continuously enhances generation quality over time. 

**Task Space Definition.** Formally, the generation process requires the specification of a robot embodiment, denoted as _r ∈R_ , which is selected from three primary categories: single-arm robots, dual-arm robots, and mobile dual-arm robots. To define the semantic boundaries of the generation space, the system accepts a global scenario set _E_ , an object library _O_ , and a skill library _S_ . These definitions can be provided by the user as structured text files to align with specific hardware availability or data collection needs. 

**Scenario and Library Configuration.** In our specific implementation, we define _|E|_ = 8 distinct scenario categories, encompassing Domestic, Office, Education, Laboratory, Kitchen, Industry, Retail, and Medical environments. The scenario category _e_ serves as a high-level physical context for task generation rather than a learned decision boundary. For single-arm and dual-arm manipulation, it corresponds to the tabletop workspace setup in which the objects are arranged and manipulated. For mobile manipulation, it corresponds to the physical environment in which the robot navigates and performs the task, such as a kitchen, office, warehouse, or medical room. These scenario categories are manually specified as part of the task-generation taxonomy and are used to constrain object and skill selection toward semantically coherent and physically executable tasks. The object library _O_ comprises 1,137 distinct physical entities, while the skill library _S_ contains 118 manipulation primitives. This task space allows for high flexibility; users can customize the libraries based on their specific experimental setup or leverage Large Foundation Models (LFMs) to autonomously expand the semantic space. Additionally, RoboGene provides a remark interface for natural language constraints, allowing users to input specific requirements such as “generate long-horizon tasks composed of 3-4 sub-goals” or “prioritize tasks requiring dexterous manipulation,” which function as soft prompts during the generation phase. 

**Algorithmic Workflow.** The core execution logic of RoboGene is formalized in Algorithm 1. The process is structured as an iterative loop that transitions through sampling, generation, reflection, and refinement phases to produce valid task instances. We initialize the system by setting usage counters _u_ ( _·_ ) and history statistics _H_ to zero. In the _Diversity-Driven Sampling_ phase, the system identifies the target scenario _et_ with the minimum usage count to prioritize under-represented environments. It then filters context-relevant objects _Oet_ and skills _Set_ based on semantic similarity, subsequently sampling candidate sets _Ot_ and _St_ using the LFU strategy. 

Following sampling, the generator Φ _gen_ synthesizes an initial task proposal _Traw_ based on the robot type _r_ and sampled constraints. This proposal immediately enters the _Self-Reflection Evaluation_ phase, where it is scrutinized by three evaluators to produce specific critiques: _fnov_ for novelty, _fcon_ for constraint adherence, and _femb_ for Embodied Plausibility. In the _Memory-Augmented Refinement_ phase, the system retrieves relevant heuristic guidelines _v_ from the long-term memory _M_ .

### **D Evaluation Metrics and Protocol** 

To rigorously validate the efficacy of the tasks generated by our framework, we establish a comprehensive benchmarking protocol. We compare **RoboGene** against four distinct baselines: a standard Rule-based method, Human expert design, and two state-of-the-art Large Foundation Models (LFMs), specifically GPT-4o [21] and Gemini 2.5 Pro [22]. To ensure statistical significance, we generated a diverse evaluation corpus consisting of 900 tasks for each method, balanced evenly across three embodiment categories: 300 single-arm, 300 dual-arm, and 300 mobile manipulation tasks. Given that quantifying the quality of open-ended robotic tasks is an under-explored challenge, we propose a novel suite of multi-dimensional metrics designed to assess linguistic clarity, logical coherence, asset grounding, and physical executability. The three judgment-based metrics are independently assessed by human evaluators and Claude Opus 5 in Table 5, and the main text reports the average of the two evaluations. 

**Task Clarity.** This metric evaluates the linguistic quality of the generated instruction. It assesses whether the task description is concise, unambiguous, and free from excessive rhetoric, ensuring interpretability by human operators during data collection. To mitigate subjective bias, we employ a hybrid evaluation strategy. We aggregate binary judgments (0 or 1) from two distinct evaluators, including Claude Opus 5 and human experts, and calculate the final score as the average of these two independent assessments. 

**Robot Type Consistency.** This metric verifies the alignment between the generated task description and the specified robot embodiment constraints _r_ . A penalty is assigned if the task requirements contradict the robot’s kinematic configuration, such as assigning a bimanual coordination task to a single-arm robot. Similar to task clarity, this score is derived by human evaluators and Claude Opus 5 using the same binary rubric. 

**Logical Validity.** This metric determines if the task description adheres to real-world semantic and common-sense constraints. It penalizes contextually inappropriate actions, such as performing industrial machining operations within a domestic dining environment. This metric is assessed by human evaluators and Claude Opus 5 using the same binary rubric.

### **E Implementation Details of Baselines** 

To ensure a rigorous comparative analysis, we provide detailed implementations of the baseline methods employed in our evaluation. These baselines represent the spectrum of task generation approaches, ranging from rule-based and human-based algorithms to state-of-the-art foundation models operating without agentic scaffolding. 

**Rule-based Method.** This baseline utilizes a deterministic algorithm structured around a triplenested loop. The system systematically iterates through the alphabetized lists of valid scenarios _E_ , objects _O_ , and skills _S_ to mechanically synthesize task tuples. While this exhaustive combinatorial approach maximizes the theoretical coverage of the search space, it lacks semantic filtering capabilities. Consequently, the method produces a high volume of logically incoherent tasks, such as “cooking a wrench,” which are physically infeasible. Furthermore, the generated instructions are primarily limited to simple, single-step primitive actions, failing to capture the complexity required for generalist robot learning. 

**Human-based Method.** To evaluate manual curation, we recruited expert annotators to generate tasks, granting them access to the complete history of previously designed entries to encourage diversity in distribution. However, our observations identified two critical limitations in this approach. First, human designers exhibited significant cognitive bias, disproportionately favoring familiar settings such as Domestic and Kitchen environments while neglecting specialized domains like Industry or Medical scenarios. This tendency reflects an inherent difficulty for humans to conceive tasks within unfamiliar physical contexts. Second, the designers rapidly encountered a creativity plateau, where subsequent generations devolved into minor variations of existing tasks, such as simple object substitution or positional adjustments, rather than introducing structurally novel behaviors. 

**GPT-4o.** For the GPT-4o baseline, we leveraged the prompt engineering capabilities of the model by directly injecting the complete sets of definitions for scenarios _E_ , objects _O_ , and skills _S_ into the context window. The model was instructed to function as an autonomous task generator, outputting tasks strictly in the required JSON format. Crucially, this baseline operates in an open-loop manner, lacking the feedback mechanisms, self-reflection, or external memory modules present in our framework.
