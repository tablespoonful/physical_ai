# 2 METHOD 

**Problem Formulation.** We consider the problem of learning a VLA policy that maps multimodal observations to sequences of robot actions. At each time step, the policy receives three inputs: an RGB image _It ∈_ R<sup>_H×W ×_3</sup> , a proprioceptive state _st ∈_ R<sup>_ds_</sup> , and a language instruction _l ∈L_ . The output is an action sequence of horizon length _H_ , denoted as _At_ : _t_ + _H_ = ( _at, at_ +1 _, . . . , at_ + _H−_ 1) _._ Instead of predicting continuous actions directly, we represent each action chunk as a sequence of discrete codes ( _z_ 1 _, . . . , zN_ ) obtained via a VQ tokenizer. The policy is then trained in an autoregressive manner to generate these codes conditioned on ( _It, st, l_ ). The VQ tokenizer subsequently decodes the generated codes back into continuous actions. 

A key bottleneck of autoregressive VLA models lies in the token sequence length. Since tokens are generated sequentially, each requiring a full forward pass through a large transformer, inference latency grows quadratically due to attention complexity with respect to sequence length. Moreover, correct decoding requires all tokens to be predicted accurately, making the model brittle when token lengths vary. This issue is particularly acute in whole-body control, where high degrees of freedom lead to long action sequences. For example, the FAST tokenizer requires 150–200 tokens to represent a 2-second motion, which corresponds to an inference delay of roughly three seconds. Our experiments with policies initialized from multiple VLMs further confirm that training a VLA on variable-length codes is substantially more challenging than on fixed-length representations. To overcome these limitations, we propose **FASTerVQ** and **FASTerVLA** , which compress action representations, reduce the effective autoregressive depth, and equip the policy with a lightweight expert pathway attuned to action-level structure. The next sections unfold these components in detail.

Real-world rollouts of **FASTerVLA** are shown in Figure 15. **492** 4 RELATED WORK **493 Vision-Language-Action models.** With advances in LLMs and multimodal systems (Sun et al., **494** 2024; Achiam et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2023; Dubey et al., 2024), Vision-Language-Action (VLA) **495** models have emerged as a promising direction for universal manipulation, leveraging the scalability **496** of pretrained VLMs (Brohan et al., 2023; Driess et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024; **497** Pertsch et al., 2025; Intelligence et al., 2025). Continuous control is typically modeled through **498** either diffusion-based methods (Bjorck et al., 2025; Black et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025) or autore- **499** gressive prediction over discretized actions (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Pertsch et al., **500** 2025). Although autoregressive VLAs offer strong language grounding and generalization, they re- **501** main slower than non-autoregressive approaches (Brohan et al., 2023; Pertsch et al., 2025; Black **502** et al., 2024). Our method instead learns a compact, structured discrete action space that aligns with **503** pretrained VLMs, allowing autoregressive policies to operate at a more efficient token granularity **504** and bringing inference speed into a practically usable regime. 

**505 VQ Tokenizer.** VQ tokenization is widely used for modal compression across images (Yu et al., **506** 2024; Sargent et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2024; Bao et al., 2022), video (Yu et al., 2023; Tang et al., **507** 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a), audio (Casanova et al., 2024; Parker et al., 2025; **508** Zhang et al., 2023; Lahrichi et al., 2025), graphs (Wang et al., 2025a; Zeng et al., 2025; Nguyen **509** et al., 2024), and multimodal fusion (Sadok et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b). Audio **510** codecs and action tokenizers share key traits: both process continuous time-series with short-term **511** fluctuations, long-term trends, and periodic patterns (Parker et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2025); both face non-uniform information density (Parker et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023); and both require strong **512** temporal causality to ensure sequence coherence (Lahrichi et al., 2025). FASTerVQ therefore adopts **513** an audio-inspired residual architecture, and our experiments confirm that, with sufficient data, such **514** VQ designs inherit the strong generalization and scaling behavior observed across modalities. **515 516 Action tokenization.** Autoregressive VLAs require discretizing continuous actions. Early approaches serialize each action dimension (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024), while later meth- **517** ods flatten full action chunks into 1D sequences (Zhao et al., 2023; Chi et al., 2024; Pertsch et al., **518** 2025; Belkhale & Sadigh, 2024) using relative (Black et al., 2024; Intelligence et al., 2025) or **519** delta actions (Pertsch et al., 2025). Existing tokenizers—binning (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., **520** 2024), DCT+BPE (Pertsch et al., 2025), and VQ variants (Belkhale & Sadigh, 2024; Wang et al., **521** 2025c)—either under-compress, over-fragment, or lack reconstruction fidelity, producing action vo- **522** cabularies that remain challenging for AR VLAs to model. **523** 

**Block-wise generation.** Chunk-wise generation appears across modalities: long-video models syn- **524** thesize segments sequentially, conditioning each chunk on the last frame of the previous one (Zhang **525** & Lim, 2024; Yang et al., 2025b), speech models adopt dynamic chunk-wise prediction for faster au- **526** toregressive synthesis (Li et al., 2025), and language models explore multi-token decoding or block- **527** wise diffusion to reduce sequential depth (Li et al., 2024b; Arriola et al., 2025). In robot control, **528** chunk policies (Zhao et al., 2023) are induced to mitigate compounding error, while (Zhang et al., **529** 2025) show that chunked causal transformers further reduce autoregressive steps. (Kim et al., 2025) **530** also performs parallel chunk decoding. Our approach instead performs block-wise autoregression **531** over discrete action codes, enabling fast multi-token prediction while preserving AR consistency. 

> **532** 5 CONCLUSION **533** We propose an efficient autoregressive VLA framework that couples a flexible vector quantization **534** module with a efficient VLA training inference setting. It delivers strong task performance with **535** low-latency inference and generalizes across backbones, as the pretrained VQ can be reused in **536** downstream tasks without retraining. The success stems from three key ideas: a codec-inspired **537** RVQ balancing accuracy, diversity, and code length; block-wise autoregressive decoding for faster, **538** expressive inference; and a lightweight mixture-of-experts VLA for action tokens. These results **539** show that autoregressive modeling can be fast, transferable, and scalable for multimodal generation.

## A.3 ABLATION STUDY 

**943 FASTerVQ Ablations.** As shown in Table 6, FASTERVQ exhibits clear performance trends across **944** the three ablated dimensions. The TAAE tokenizer delivers the strongest reconstruction fidelity, **945** reflected by the lowest _ℓ_ 1 loss, and also reaches the highest success rate, surpassing both the CNN **946** and Transformer variants. Increasing the codebook size improves representation capacity up to 4096 **947** entries, at which point success rate and utilization peak before degrading at 8192, indicating the onset **948** of codebook collapse. Finally, residual quantization yields the most stable gains with two to three **949** residual layers, whereas deeper stacks introduce diminishing returns or mild instability. Together, **950** these observations support the final choice adopted in our design: a TAAE tokenizer coupled with a **951** 4096-entry codebook and three residual codebooks, which jointly provide the most favorable balance between reconstruction quality and policy success rate. **952 953 FASTerVLA Ablations.** We examine two essential components of **VLA** : the action expert (AE) **954** responsible for action tokenization, and the block-wise autoregressive (BAR) decoding mechanism. **955** Table 7 reveals that on **libero** , AE consistently enhances SR relative to the no-AE baseline, with **956** pretraining providing the most pronounced gain (97.9 vs. 95.5). On **simpler-widow** , AE trained **957** from scratch collapses (23.6), suggesting that gradients from the VLM offer only a faint signal for learning a high-quality action tokenizer. Pretraining therefore becomes indispensable, lifting **958** SR to 87.9. These observations naturally motivate a two-stage training scheme: first pretrain AE **959** within the VLM, then fine-tune jointly with partial freezing. This schedule accelerates convergence **960** and markedly stabilizes optimization. For decoding, BAR substantially improves both accuracy and **961** efficiency: SR rises from 95.5 to 96.7, while latency is reduced by more than 2 _×_ (323,ms to 140,ms). **962** When BAR is combined with AE, SR further increases to 97.7 at the same low latency, offering the **963** most harmonious balance between precision and speed. **964** We also ablate different BAR block sizes. Our intuition is that BAR becomes effective when the **965** action-token sequence exhibits a relatively stable length and when meaningful correlations are pre- **966** served across tokens. Under such conditions, BAR can be applied directly and remains reliable. **967** We evaluate multiple BAR block sizes using the same FASTerVQ configuration with an action code **968** length of 42. As shown in Appendix A.3, performance remains stable as long as the block size lies **969** within a reasonable multiple of the action-dimension code length. When the block size matches this **970** code length, the result is the strongest. Empirically, these findings echo our design intuition: BAR **971** integrates seamlessly with FASTerVQ because FASTerVQ preserves the latent structure of action chunks, allowing BAR to exploit this structure for coherent block-wise generation.
