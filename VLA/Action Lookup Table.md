**039** Imitation learning for robot manipulation requires training a policy to map from image inputs to **040** action sequence outputs given a relatively small number of demonstrations. Recently, the Diffusion **041** Policy Chi et al. (2023) has emerged as a powerful approach to this problem by modeling the robot’s **042** policy as a denoising diffusion probabilistic model Ho et al. (2020). The Diffusion Policy infers **043** an action chunk (a short action sequence, typically 16 time steps long) conditioned on the robot’s **044** camera views and joint angles. To infer an action chunk, the policy iteratively denoises a random **045** action chunk using a learned denoising filter. The denoised action chunk is applied to the robot (typ- **046** ically executing 8 of the 16 actions in the chunk), and the loop repeats. The architecture is derived **047** directly from a denoising diffusion model for image generation Saharia et al. (2022b); Rombach **048** et al. (2022), adapted to produce action chunk outputs. The primary advantage of the Diffusion **049** Policy lies in its ability to model multi-modal action distributions, scale to high-dimensional robot **050** action spaces, and produce long-horizon action sequences. Indeed, recent studies have shown that diffusion policies outperform many existing methods on challenging manipulation benchmarks Ze **051** et al. (2024); Wang et al. **064** In this paper we show that, indeed, diffusion policies highly overfit the training data, such that they essentially recall training action chunks at inference. One of the most common solutions is to use remote controllers, such **762** as VR, 3D space mouse, or smartphones. However, due to high latency and indirect operation, the **763** data collected in this way is often messy and low-quality, making it difficult to accurately capture **764** human skills. To further understand the role of representation quality in ALT, we evaluated the system under five different encoder architectures—ResNet-18, SimpleCNN, ViT, CLIP, and Swin—while varying their feature dimensions. The results in Table 4 reveal several consistent trends. First, ResNet-18 achieves uniformly high performance across all dimensions, reflecting the stability and strong inductive biases of convolutional networks, which are well suited for manipulation-oriented visual inputs. In contrast, SimpleCNN and ViT perform significantly worse: the former lacks sufficient representational capacity, while the latter requires large-scale pretraining and offers limited spatial inductive bias, making its embeddings unstable in our domain. CLIP produces strong features but is highly sensitive to output dimensionality—dimensions that are too small under-represent the feature manifold, whereas excessively large dimensions distort it, leading to degraded contrastive alignment. Finally, Swin exhibits a clear performance improvement as dimensionality increases, consistent with its hierarchical, local-window design that combines CNN-like locality with transformer expressiveness. Overall, these results highlight that encoder choice and representation quality have a substantial impact on ALT performance, confirming that representation quality is a critical factor for contrastive training.
