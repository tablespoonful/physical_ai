## 1 INTRODUCTION 

**029** In recent times, we have seen significant strides in the language-following and generalization capa- **030** bilities of robotic manipulation policies (Brohan et al. (2023); Intelligence et al. (2025); Kim et al. **031** (2024); NVIDIA et al. (2025)). While these policies are getting better for real-world deployment, a critical limitation remains: the absence of long-term memory. Memory allows humans to handle the **032** inherent partial observability found in their environment. For instance, if a person wanted to make a **033** sandwich, they would have to recall where they saw the jar of peanut butter or the knife, especially if **034** these items were not recently viewed. The ability to form and retrieve long-term visual memories is **035** a crucial step towards robots solving complex, multi-step tasks. The goal of this paper is to provide **036** an effective way to enable existing generalist policies to solve tasks that require long-term visual **037** memory. **038** Because conditioning on long sequences of high-dimensional image and video sequences is compu- **039** tationally expensive, many existing generalist end-to-end policies are trained with little to no visual **040** history. The high memory cost makes training prohibitively expensive and model deployment un- **041** usably slow. Furthermore, long observation histories can often introduce a form of overfitting — **042** shortcut reliance on spurious correlations between inputs and demonstrator actions (Torne et al., **043** 2025). The policy misgeneralizes under its own state distribution, leading to performance degrada- **044** tion during deployment due to compounding covariate shift between states visited by the demon- **045** strator policy and the learned policy. The suboptimal policy will generate histories that differ from **046** those seen by the expert, which is only made worse as observation history lengthens. **047** Some past works have shown it is possible to expand the observation context of their policy via **048** auxiliary losses (Torne et al., 2025), or by finetuning pretrained foundation models for action pre- **049** diction with native memory capabilities (Fang et al., 2025). Although these methods significantly **050** increase the types of tasks a robot can execute, they are challenging to naively scale to long histo- **051** ries. To overcome this, policies must learn to filter out the task-relevant information from the full **052** historical context to prevent the memory footprint from exploding on tasks that require long-range **053** dependencies.

**054** To this end, we propose approaching long-term memory for robotic policies with a hierarchical **055** framework. The high-level policy is a fine-tuned video-understanding VLM trained to output action **056** subtasks and, most importantly, to select keyframes from its fixed recent context that represent **057** important information it will need to remember to solve the task. The low-level policy is a generalist **058** robot policy fine-tuned to execute the subtasks specified by the high-level policy. Together, the low- **059** level policy handles the robot-specific challenges of the task that require high-frequency inference **060** such as kinematic control, and the high-level policy manages planning and memory-specific aspects **061** of the task such as deciding what object or tool to manipulate next based on the high-level task **062** and its memory. We take advantage of the fact that these open-source VLMs are finetuned on large **063** amounts of video understanding data. With this strong prior, we find that we only need a relatively small number of teleoperated robot demonstrations with additional annotations to adapt these VLMs **064** to accomplish robot-specific memory-based tasks (Bai et al., 2025). **065**

## 2 RELATED WORK 

**074 Memory in Robotics and Long-Context Policies.** Memory is essential for generalist robots to **075** complete complex tasks. Prior work primarily studies memory in the context of comparatively **076** short-horizon tasks. For example, Torne et al. (2025) and Fang et al. (2025) use different approaches **077** to extend the context of imitation policies from a few frames to at most two dozen. Our work in- **078** vestigate tasks that require building memory from hundreds of frames. Unlike previous approaches, our method can choose to include task-relevant frames in the context spanning the entire episode. **079** Another body of work investigates the compression of images in the policy’s context. Li et al. **080** (2025a) compresses similar observations in pixel space, which is effective for stationary camera se- **081** tups but struggles with wrist-mounted cameras that are necessary for most dexterous manipulation **082** tasks. Memory has also played a major role in robotic navigation research. Some navigation works **083** represent memory with an explicit geometric and/or semantic map of the environment Henry et al. **084** (2012); Yu et al. (2024). However, spatial maps of the environment are hard to apply in manipulation **085** tasks since the robot often modifies the environment. Other works directly prompt API-based VLMs **086** with video context to decide where the robot should navigate (Chiang et al., 2024; Sharma et al., **087** 2023; Chen et al., 2024). We found that existing API-based VLMs are not sufficient to reason about **088** robot affordances for our long-horizon, memory-aware tasks (Table 2), so we resort to finetuning **089** open-weight models. 

**090 Foundation Models and Long-Horizon Tasks in Robotics.** Recent progress in vision-language- **091** action models (VLAs) have allowed for impressive generality in robotics. VLAs combine web-scale **092** pretraining with expressive action decoding mechanisms to execute real-world tasks. Conceptually, **093** two paradigms have emerged. The first is a single end-to-end model that directly maps images and a **094** language task to actions (Intelligence et al., 2025; Brohan et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2025). The second **095** is a hierarchical approach that uses a high-level policy to output an intermediate representation to guide a low-level policy. Possible intermediate representations include latent embeddings (Shentu **096** et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024), language subtasks (Shi et al., 2025; 2024), and way- **097** points (Li et al., 2025b). Prior work has shown that hierarchical approaches improve performance **098** on long-horizon tasks (Shi et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2025) by introducing a tempo- **099** ral abstraction: a high-level policy operates at a lower frequency and decomposes a complex task **100** into simpler subgoals (Zhang et al., 2023). The low-level policy can then focus on high-frequency, **101** reactive motor control to achieve the current subgoal. Other methods that integrate LLM-based **102** high-level planning with task and motion planning, imitation learning, and RL (Dalal et al., 2024; **103** Zhou et al., 2024) similarly tackle long-horizon manipulation via task decompositions, but rely on **104** a well-specified planning stack and do not provide an explicit long-term memory mechanism over raw visual histories. **105 106** Our work builds on the second paradigm, using language subtasks as the intermediate representation. **107** What distinguishes MemER from prior hierarchical approaches is the addition of a stable, persistent

**Video Keyframe Selection.** Outside of robotics, previous work in computer vision has also studied incorporating longer contexts for VLMs (Goletto et al. (2024); Manigrasso et al. (2025)). ## 5 DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK 

**505** 

**506** We introduced MemER, a hierarchical vision–language–action framework that _scales memory via_ **507** _experience retrieval_ . A high-level memory policy processes streamed observations, nominates **508** keyframes to retain, and emits language subtasks that a low-level controller executes. A simple on- **509** line consolidation strategy converts per-timestep candidate keyframes into a compact, stable episodic **510** memory that is fed back into the high-level policy. Across three real-world, long-horizon manipulation domains, MemER significantly improves performance on tasks requiring minutes of recall **511** while retaining low-latency inference and strong compatibility with existing VLA backbones. **512** 

**513** Despite its benefits, our approach has several limitations. We continuously accumulate informative **514** keyframes but currently lack a mechanism to discard them when they become too numerous—an **515** issue that may arise for tasks requiring hours of memory. Enabling the high-level policy to reason **516** about which keyframes to not only _add_ but also _delete_ for modifiable long-term memory is an exciting direction for future work. Aghajohari et al. (2025) proposes an approach that uses reinforcement **517** learning to train an LLM to maintain a fixed-size memory state throughout its chain-of-thought **518** reasoning. Adapting this idea could provide a promising way to endow MemER with a learned **519** memory management system that includes an explicit _forgetting_ mechanism. In addition, our mem- **520** ory is limited to visual observations; incorporating other sensory modalities such as tactile or audio **521** is a promising extension. Finally, we study a single robot embodiment, and extending to mobile **522** manipulation and multi-room tasks, where memory must interleave spatial mapping with episodic **523** recall, would bring the system closer to human-like memory. We view MemER as a step toward **524** robot policies that _decide what to remember_ and _leverage those memories when needed_ for effective **525** long-horizon control. **526**
